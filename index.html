FIRST WARN (disruptive, not necessarily dangerous): daytime (6 AM–8 PM CT) NWS gridpoint hourly PoP >50% (beyond the NWS grid or if NWS fails: >50% of models with ≥0.01" daytime precip; commute windows 6–9 AM / 4–7 PM noted) · dense fog: NWS gridpoint visibility ≤0.25 mi or Dense Fog Advisory (model visibility is not in our model feed) · gusts ≥40 mph (NWS gridpoint or model-median peak) · high drops ≥20°F day to day · light wintry mix without accumulation (NWS weather likely+, or ≥half the models) · Wind/Frost/Dense Smoke advisories.

.DISCUSSION...
Issued at 243 AM CDT Sat Oct 31 2026

Water vapor imagery shows another shortwave trough moving northeast 
through Oklahoma currently. Forcing from this shortwave should 
result in an increase in coverage of showers late this morning/early 
this afternoon across eastern Kansas and west-central Missouri. This 
activity then lifts to the north and east with time through the 
afternoon and evening hours. While it doesn't look like a washout, 
it does look like there will be isolated to scattered showers across 
the area at some point through the day today/ this evening. So 
anyone doing outside activities may need an umbrella with them.
Fortunately, the chances for storms looks low with only weak  
instability available. Overnight, as that shortwave starts to 
move northeast of the area and we move into the subsident 
regime, showers will decrease in coverage and eventually come to
an end. Sunday, shortwave ridging builds through the area and 
that should keep the area dry as the next system moves through 
the Western US. 

The Monday system is much stronger than the previous shortwaves. 
Strong southerly flow ahead of this system will result in very 
strong moisture transport into the area. In fact, precipitable water 
values are at the max values for the NAEFS climatological period 
across northeastern Kansas and northwestern Missouri. So this is 4 
to 5 standard deviations above normal for this time of year. 
Additionally, there is potential eastern Kansas and western Missouri 
are in the left front quadrant of the upper-jet moving into the area 
ahead of the strong shortwave. Strong flow through much of the 
troposphere will result in strong wind shear, potentially in excess 
of 50kts. Instability, though weak, is available in this strongly 
forced environment and we continue to have the potential for a high-
shear, low-CAPE severe weather event. There are a couple scenarios 
that cold unfold here. Storms could develop in the warm sector over 
eastern Kansas, well ahead of the front. This looks possible,
though unlikely, based on some of the forecast soundings in 
that area showing minimal or no CIN by the afternoon hours. A 
more plausible scenario is that storms develop along or just 
ahead of the front/ dryline well west of the area and then 
track east into the area later in the evening or overnight 
hours. There could also be some combination of both of these 
with storms developing earlier in the warm sector, then a line 
of storms moving into the area in association with the front, 
though this also seems less likely. Any one of these would have
the potential for strong to severe storms and given the strong 
kinematics, strong to damaging winds will be the most likely 
hazard. Strong 0-3km shear may also support a tornado risk as 
well if a mature line of storms moves into the area. The latest 
Day 3 Convective Outlook brings a marginal risk of severe 
weather to eastern Kansas and western Missouri. A slight risk 
clips the extreme southwestern portions of the forecast area. 

In the wake of this system, temperatures trend closer to normal for 
the later half of next week. Normal highs are in the upper 50s so 
even though we cool down, highs still look to be a few degrees above 
normal with highs forecast to be in the lower to middle 60s.
